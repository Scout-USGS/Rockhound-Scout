The best spots aren’t on anybody’s list yet. They’re in the geology, and Rockhound Scout reads it. We checked the Three Rivers–George West area for petrified wood in publicly accessible areas: a leading rockhounding app didn’t show a single stop. Rockhound Scout found 4.

How it works

  1. Name a place anywhere in the U.S. A town, landmark, or your hotel’s address works. Add what you’re hunting if you know: “agate near Quartzsite, AZ,” “creek crossings near Keokuk, IA,” or “anything on Hwy 101 south of Newport, OR.” Not sure what’s there? Just ask “What can I find near Llano, TX?”
  2. The app reads the maps. It surveys which rocks lie in your area and what each one is known to produce, then checks the geologic map along every road and finds the creek crossings that sit in the best rock. Creeks cut down through the formation and wash the good stuff out — that’s where minerals, fossils, and petrified wood concentrate.
  3. You get hotspot cards. Each card gives the rock at the spot, the creek or river crossed, driving directions from a nearby town, what to look for from the truck window, likely finds, and a GPS point for Waze or Google Maps. You can also print your Hotspot Cards on paper or save them as a PDF.

Where does the data come from?

Rock formation data comes from Macrostrat, a public database that compiles published geologic maps from sources including the U.S. Geological Survey and state geological surveys such as the Texas Bureau of Economic Geology. Roads, creeks, and lakes come from U.S. Census Bureau and USGS maps, public land from the USGS Protected Areas Database, and place lookups from OpenStreetMap. On top of those, Rockhound Scout keeps its own map library built from these sources, and its own field list — what each rock formation in all 50 states is known to produce, compiled from published geology and my own time in the field. The app generates remarkable results and interesting details about the Hotspots it identifies.
